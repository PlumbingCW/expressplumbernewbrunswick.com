Basement water is rising and the pump is not keeping up

Treat this as urgent. Our dispatch line, (732) 466-1098, is open 24/7. While you wait, check the pump's plug and the breaker, and look at the float. If the float is stuck against the pit wall, lifting it by hand may start the pump.

Do not wade into a flooded basement if the water is near outlets, a furnace, a panel or a freezer. Shut off power to the basement at the main panel only if you can reach it from dry ground. If you are unsure, stay upstairs and call.

  • Move valuables and stored boxes off the floor if the water is still shallow.
  • Do not run a wet-dry vacuum or extension cord through standing water.
  • If a sewage smell comes from the pit or floor drain, avoid contact. It may be an ejector pit, not a sump.
  • Take photos of the water level and anything damaged before cleanup.

A pump that cycles every minute on a dry day may be pulling groundwater from a bigger problem. A plumber can tell the difference between a pump issue and the water coming in.

Power outages, storms and battery backup

Heavy rain and power cuts tend to arrive together, which is exactly when a plug-in pump goes quiet. A battery backup pump sits in the same pit and takes over when the primary stops or the grid drops.

A backup has limits. The battery lasts a set number of hours under load, so ask how long yours is rated for. Test it each season by pouring water into the pit and confirming both pumps start.

Sump pump or sewage ejector

Sump pump

Moves groundwater from a pit near the foundation out and away from the house. Clean water, usually little solids.

Sewage ejector

Lifts wastewater from a basement bathroom or laundry up to the sewer line. It handles solids and sits in a sealed pit.

Why it matters

They are different pumps. A sump pump in an ejector pit will clog, and an ejector pit should never be uncovered or drained into the yard.

Installing a new pit and pump

According to the NJ Division of Consumer Affairs plumbing board, a permit has to be signed by the licensed plumber or a bona-fide representative. A homeowner cannot simply have someone else pull it, so ask who is signing for your pit and pump work.

  1. Assess the waterThe plumber looks at where water collects and whether a pit already exists.
  2. Size the pumpHorsepower and lift depend on how high the water must go and how fast it enters.
  3. Set the pit and check valveThe pump sits level on a stable base. A check valve on the discharge keeps pumped water from flowing back into the pit.
  4. Route the dischargePipe goes out of the house and away from the foundation. This step has local rules, covered below.
  5. Test and demonstrateThe plumber fills the pit, confirms the float works and shows you the reset points.

A new pump is a fixed installation job in the same family as other plumbing installation work, with power and discharge planned together.

Source: NJ Division of Consumer Affairs, State Board of Examiners of Master Plumbers FAQ, njconsumeraffairs.gov/plu/Pages/FAQ.aspx

Where the discharge goes: ask before you route it

We do not have a sourced New Brunswick ordinance on sump discharge, and we will not state one from memory. Many municipalities limit where pumped water may go, including whether it may enter a sanitary sewer. Ask the building department before the work is planned.

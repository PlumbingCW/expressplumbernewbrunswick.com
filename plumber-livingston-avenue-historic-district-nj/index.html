Where the district sits

The Livingston Avenue Historic District covers about 32 acres along Livingston Avenue, between Hale Street and Morris Street. It was added to the National Register of Historic Places in February 1996. The nomination describes buildings stretched along an eight-block span that begins at the edge of downtown.

Cross streets in the nomination include Morris, Welton, Townsend, Seaman and Baldwin. When you call, give the cross street as well as the house number. Livingston Avenue is long enough that a number alone can leave a dispatcher guessing which end you mean.

Source: National Park Service, National Register nomination for the Livingston Avenue Historic District, and Wikipedia for listing dates

What the nomination says about the buildings

The district's period of significance runs from 1870 to 1929, and the nomination counts 58 contributing buildings. Most dwellings are described as two-and-a-half-story frame houses. Two five-story masonry apartment buildings, Livingston Manor and the Brunswick Arms, date from the late 1920s.

Frame houses

A tall house with bathrooms stacked over each other shares drain lines between floors. A clog upstairs can show up as a slow drain below.

Divided houses

The nomination found many houses converted to offices or split into apartments. In a divided building, one shutoff or one water heater may serve more than one unit.

Apartment blocks

In a five-story building a leak can travel down several floors before anyone sees it, so report ceiling stains early.

Carriage houses and garages

Outbuildings behind the main houses sometimes have their own water line or floor drain. Mention any detached space when you describe the job.

Source: National Register nomination, period of significance 1870 to 1929

Old does not mean failing

Nobody can tell what is inside a wall from the year on a building. Some pipes in older houses have been replaced over the decades, and some have not. A sewer camera inspection shows what a drain line looks like, and it settles a lot of guessing.

If a plumber does find worn supply lines, repiping can be done in stages so that rooms stay usable. Ask for the scope and the price by phone before any wall is opened.

Calls this kind of housing tends to raise

  • A slow or gurgling drain that affects more than one fixture, which points to a shared line and a possible drain cleaning job.
  • A water heater squeezed into a closet or basement corner where access is tight, covered under water heater repair.
  • A stain on plaster or a ceiling with no visible pipe, which needs leak tracing before anything is cut.
  • A tenant with no hot water who needs to know what to tell the landlord.

A quick checklist for an older house

  • Run each tap for a minute and watch for weak flow or brown water at start-up.
  • Check under sinks and around toilet bases for damp wood or soft flooring.
  • Look at the water heater for rust at the base, pooled water or a tank that heats slowly.
  • Pour a bucket of water into the basement floor drain and watch how fast it clears.
  • Note the date and what you found, so a plumber can see what changed.

Before you start work in a historic building

  1. Ask the cityCall the New Brunswick building department and ask whether your job needs a permit and whether the building's historic status adds any review.
  2. Know your shutoffsFind the main shutoff and any fixture valves before something fails. In divided houses, ask which valve controls which unit.
  3. Photograph firstTake photos of the area before work. They help the plumber, the owner and any insurer.
  4. Get the scope by phoneAsk what will be opened, what will be patched and who is responsible for plaster or tile repair afterward.

Renters, landlords and who calls

About 21 percent of New Brunswick housing units are owner-occupied, according to Census data for 2020 to 2024. That means most repair calls involve a tenant and an owner. If you rent, tell the dispatcher so, because approval may have to come from the landlord.
